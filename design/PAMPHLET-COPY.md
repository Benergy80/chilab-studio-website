# Pamphlet copy contract

## Thesis

Every project is an opportunity to explore meaning. The studio's work is to
explore materials and connect them to that meaning.

So a pamphlet is not a spec sheet and not an artist statement. It carries both
halves and shows the hinge between them: what the work means, what that meaning
demanded of the material, and what the studio did to answer it.

## The four beats

Each project's `story` in `content/projects.json` is four sections, in order:

1. **The work.** What it is and what it is about. The meaning, plainly. Where a
   collaborating artist framed it, use their framing and credit it.
2. **The material question.** The hinge. What the meaning asked of the material
   -- the demand the idea placed on physical stuff. This beat is why the pamphlet
   exists; without it the other three are a catalog entry and a wall label.
3. **The making.** How the studio explored materials and processes to answer the
   question. Tooling, molds, mixes, cuts, finishes, the things that went wrong.
4. **In place.** What it became: installed, shipped, lived with. The studio's
   own stake -- what the job cost, what it was worth -- lands here.

Labels are per project, not boilerplate. "Doing it hundreds of times" beats
"The making" when that is the truth of the job. The four beats stay in order.

## Voice

`design/DESIGN.md` governs. Workshop verbs: build, cast, finish, model, restore,
install, coordinate. No "elevate", "seamless", "revolutionize", no hype metrics.

The old Claude Design decks run lyrical -- "a still life made monument". Harvest
their substance, not their register.

Write plainly about difficulty and stakes. "That is a humbling audience" is in
voice. "We pushed the boundaries of craftsmanship" is not.

## Facts

Materials, credits, dates and places come from the project record; do not restate
them in prose. Never invent a quantity, a date, a name, or a process step. When a
detail is needed and unknown, leave it out or ask.
